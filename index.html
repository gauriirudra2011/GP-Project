Global Perspectives Research Project

Why do our school gates turn into traffic jams twice a day?

Picture it: the bell rings, hundreds of us spill out of the gate, and we step straight into a wall of idling cars, honking vans and scooters squeezing through gaps that were never meant for them. Younger students weave between bumpers to reach their ride. The air smells of exhaust. Neighbours wait, and so does any ambulance unlucky enough to be stuck behind us.

We are Safe Streets Squad, and we believe the journey to school should never be the most dangerous part of a child's day. Our mission is simple and urgent: to understand why this chaos happens and to push, with evidence, for school commutes that are safer, cleaner and calmer for everyone.

Abstract

This portfolio investigates traffic congestion around schools as both a local frustration and a global challenge. Drawing on a survey of 195 students, recorded interviews with our peers, and India's national guidance for safer school commutes (IRC:SP:32-2023), we trace the problem from its roots — a heavy reliance on private vehicles, road layouts that leave no room for drop-offs, and careless parking — to its very human consequences for safety, air quality and community life. We then evaluate sustainable alternatives, from walking and cycling to carpooling, school buses and the internationally celebrated School Streets model, and invite you to help us act on what we found.

The Issue

Causes & Effects: how a ten-minute drop-off becomes a daily crisis

Congestion at the school gate is not an accident of bad luck. It is the predictable result of a few choices we make every day, and it carries costs that land hardest on the people least able to protect themselves: children.

The Causes

01

Increased Use of Private Vehicles

For many families the car feels like the safest, most convenient choice, so each child arrives in a vehicle of their own. Multiply that by hundreds of students arriving within the same fifteen minutes and the road simply runs out of space. In our survey, more than a quarter of students (26.7%) travel in a private vehicle and almost half (47.7%) rely on school vans — meaning the gate is surrounded by engines long before the first bell. As one student we interviewed put it, "there are many students coming at the same time, so there are many cars around."

02

Poor Road Infrastructure

Most school roads were never designed for this rush. There are rarely dedicated drop-off bays, so cars stop wherever they can, often right at the gate. Footpaths are broken, blocked or missing altogether, pushing children onto the carriageway. Nearly a third of students who felt unsafe (31.7%) blamed poor footpaths, and 45.2% described real difficulty crossing the road. The national guidelines are clear that pick-up and drop-off points should sit at least 20 metres from the gate — yet in practice, that space simply does not exist at most schools.

03

Illegal Parking

"Just two minutes" becomes twenty when a parent double-parks, a van waits on the corner and a scooter is left on the footpath. Each vehicle narrows the road a little more until traffic behind grinds to a halt and horns start blaring. Over one in five students who felt unsafe (22.2%) pointed directly at illegal parking. It is a small act of convenience for one family, but a shared burden for everyone else on the street.

The Effects

01

Reduced Student Safety

When the kerb is full, children end up dodging moving traffic to reach their parents' car. This is not a rare sight: 48.2% of students told us they frequently see classmates walking between cars or moving vehicles, and another 45.1% see it sometimes. Speeding vehicles were the single biggest reason students felt unsafe (56.3%). The science is sobering — the IRC guidelines show that fatality risk climbs steeply once impact speeds pass 30 km/h, which is exactly why school zones are meant to stay at 20–25 km/h.

02

Increased Air Pollution

A queue of idling engines is a cloud we breathe in at nose height. Stationary vehicles release carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx) and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) right where students gather. Children breathe faster than adults and are closer to exhaust pipes, so the gate becomes one of the most polluted places in their day. It is an invisible harm, but it is repeated twice a day, every school day, for years.

03

Community Time Delays

The jam does not stay at the school. It spills onto neighbouring roads, trapping residents on their way to work, delivery drivers and, most worryingly, emergency services that cannot afford a single wasted minute. Students feel it too: nearly half of respondents (46.7%) meet congestion every day or often, and the students we interviewed told us traffic has made them late "plenty of times" — for some, at least once a week.

The most hopeful finding is also the most important: students are not attached to the car. They already know walking and cycling are better, and most would switch tomorrow. What stands in their way is not willingness — it is the fear of an unsafe road.

Sustainable Transport Solutions

Fewer engines, safer gates, healthier students

There is no single fix, but there is a clear direction: move more children with fewer vehicles. Each of the options below is already working somewhere in the world, and each speaks directly to what our survey respondents asked for.

Walking & Cycling

Active travel is the cleanest solution we have, and it gives back more than it asks. A daily walk or ride builds fitness, sharpens concentration before lessons, and lifts mood — while producing zero emissions at the gate. With 62.6% of our respondents living just 10–20 minutes away and 46.2% saying they would happily cycle, the demand is already there. What students need is continuous footpaths, protected cycle tracks and secure cycle parking, exactly as the IRC guidelines recommend.

  • Zero emissions
  • Better health
  • Lower cost

Carpooling Frameworks

When a car is truly necessary, it should rarely carry just one child. A well-organised carpool — families on the same route sharing the school run on a simple weekly rota — can take three or four cars off the road at once. Schools can make this easy by matching families by neighbourhood, reserving numbered drop-off bays for carpools, and recognising regular carpoolers. It saves parents time and fuel, and it turns a lonely commute into a friendly one.

  • Fewer cars
  • Shared costs
  • Community ties

Scaling School Bus Systems

A single school bus can replace dozens of private cars, and it delivers children together, on time, right into a safe boarding zone. Scaling up means more routes, reliable timings and pick-up points close to home, so parents trust the bus as much as their own car. The IRC guidelines add practical detail: buses should never need to reverse at pick-up, kerbs should be no higher than 150 mm with a ramp, and boarding areas should ideally sit inside the school premises.

  • High capacity
  • Supervised travel
  • Predictable timing
Global Case Study

Global Perspectives Context

School Street Programmes: giving the road back to children

Across the world, cities have tried a beautifully simple idea. For a short window at drop-off and pick-up, the road directly outside a school is temporarily closed to motor vehicles. Barriers or signs go up, a volunteer or warden stands at the entry, and for those thirty minutes the street belongs to people on foot, on bikes and on scooters. Residents and emergency vehicles can still get through, but the school run by car is no longer an option at the gate.

What makes School Streets so powerful is that it tackles every problem on this page at once. With no cars queuing at the gate, children stop dodging bumpers, idling fumes disappear from the pavement, and families discover that parking a few minutes away — or leaving the car at home entirely — works perfectly well. It is a low-cost, fast-to-trial measure that turns a chaotic bottleneck into a safe, calm space almost overnight.

Why it matters for us: our school's worst congestion comes in one short burst at pick-up (84.6%). That is exactly the kind of predictable, time-bound peak a School Street is designed to solve.

A

Traffic Rules & Regulatory Directives

Good intentions are not enough; a safe school zone needs clear rules. India's IRC:SP:32-2023 Guidelines for Safer Commute to Schools (First Revision) lays out exactly what a child-friendly street should look like: speed limits of 20–25 km/h in the School Access Zone, drop-off bays at least 20 metres from the gate, continuous footpaths separated from traffic, highlighted pedestrian waiting areas, guardrails, bollards, and clear "School Ahead" signage. Reading these guidelines, we were struck by how far our own school gate is from this standard — and how achievable it would be to close the gap.

Stakeholder Perspectives & Primary Interviews

Numbers tell us how often something happens; voices tell us how it feels. We sat down with fellow students and asked them the same honest questions: How do you get to school? Is there too much traffic? Has it made you late? Do you feel safe? And what would you change? Their answers are thoughtful, practical and, at times, worrying. What the voices have in common

Listen to all six interviews and a pattern emerges. Every student said there is too much traffic. Several have been made late by it, some as often as once a week. Those who feel safe credit the guards and wardens at the gate, which tells us supervision works; those who don't describe missing footpaths, crowded drop-offs and speeding cars — exactly the gaps the IRC guidelines identify. And their solutions, from staggered timings to separate drop-off areas and more buses and carpools, line up closely with proven global practice.

Taking It Beyond the Classroom

#StopSchoolJam: our awareness campaign

Research only matters if it reaches people, so we took our message to where students and parents actually spend their time. On Instagram, @stopschooljam4 shares simple, friendly posters with one clear ask: walk, cycle, carpool or take the bus. Our message is "Less cars. More safety. Better future." — and within our first two posts, the page grew to over 100 followers.

We kept the tone hopeful on purpose. Nobody changes their habits because they feel blamed; they change when the better option feels easy, safe and a little bit fun. Every post is a small invitation to be part of the change.
